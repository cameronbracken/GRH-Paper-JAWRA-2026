\noindent\textbf{Manuscript:} ``A real-time reservoir inflow forecast evaluation
framework'' (MS 8299185), submitted to the \emph{Journal of the American Water
Resources Association}.

\noindent\textbf{Authors:} Cameron Bracken, Youngjun Son, Vince Tidwell, and Nathalie Voisin.

\vspace{0.4em}

We thank the editor and reviewers for their comments. The main concern was that the
manuscript described a framework without identifying its contribution. We now define the
contribution as an operating procedure for comparing multiple forecasts under current 
operational conditions when hindcasts are unavailable. The revised text separates 
generic verification steps from operator feedback, metric revision, and use of operational data.

We also expanded the descriptions of the system and forecast products, added a smoothing
sensitivity analysis, characterized the evaluation period, and connected forecast quality
to the forecast value literature.

The audit also identified four errors or qualifications that required correction:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.15em]
    \item The evaluation period was given as July 2023 to May 2025. The record analyzed
    runs from June 2023 to April 2025.
    \item The three forecast products do not cover the period uniformly. Forecast B spans
    the full period; forecast A and the in-house forecast both end in November 2024. The
    metrics in Table 1 are therefore computed over overlapping periods but not identical sample sizes.
    We now report the sample size for each product and have verified that restricting the
    comparison to the 510 days on which all three are present does not change the ranking
    at any location.
    \item The paragraph comparing metrics across the three dams labeled the reported values
    as KGE when they were NSE. This is the passage Reviewer 1 asked us to check (comment
    1.6), and rewriting it is the largest change to the results.
    \item The cumulative forebay calculation was incorrect. We have corrected it and aligned
    the forecasts so the cumulative error can be compared.
    %\item An NSE skill score added during the first revision compared products over different
    %samples. The evaluated and reference forecasts must use the same observations for that
    %comparison. We removed the score and now interpret NSE together with RMSE, sample
    %coverage, and the benchmark rows.
\end{itemize}

Reviewer comments are set in indented italics, trimmed to the operative text where they are
long. A tracked-changes manuscript accompanies this response, so we quote revised text only
where the wording is itself the answer.

\section*{Editor comments}

\begin{rc}
The greatest barrier [\ldots] is the question of novelty and reproducibility. Reviewers
1, 3, and 4 note that there is no distinct statement of novelty, with reviewer 3
particularly noting that the manuscript seems to suggest that the framework is the
novelty without fully describing the novelty.
\end{rc}

We revised the introduction to state the contribution directly. The procedure addresses a
specific case where a utility must compare competing products under current operating conditions
and cannot run a hindcast. Operators select and revise metrics, and the evaluation uses the
data available in daily operations. We also distinguish this procedure from retrospective
verification and state that the two approaches are complementary.

On reproducibility, we have anonymized the proprietary data and made the forecast evaluation as
reproducible as we could given the limitations of the data. The evaluation code is public
(\url{https://github.com/HydroWIRES-PNNL/inflow-forecast-evaluation}) and sample data are
on Zenodo (\url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.16921728}). The repository now also includes a
script that reproduces every number reported in the paper from the published data. We have
added the parameter values that were previously left implicit, most importantly the smoothing
window (comment 4.1), together with a sensitivity analysis. The proprietary forecast time
series cannot be released, which we state as a limitation.

\section*{Reviewer 1}

\begin{rc}
The topic and the contents are relevant, and it is very interesting for hydropower
operators and decision-makers. However, the novelty and main contributions of this
study are unclear.
\end{rc}

We address the comments on framing, system description, tables, and figures below.


\subsection*{1.1 Novelty}

\begin{rc}
The novelty and main contributions of this study are unclear. Please explain and
discuss in detail.
\end{rc}

Please see our response to the editor for the new statement of contribution. In brief, the
contribution is a procedure for the case where a utility must choose among competing
forecast products under live operating conditions and a hindcast is not possible. What
distinguishes it from standard forecast verification is the direct operator
input in the process, and that the evaluation runs on operational data
rather than an archived dataset.

We have added to the discussion why this matters now. Forecast products using AI and
machine learning methods are being marketed to utilities at an increasing rate, and the
verification statistics vendors supply may not be verifiable. A utility generally has no
standard procedure for checking those claims against its own system, and as we now
report, often cannot inspect the model at all.

\subsection*{1.2 Persistence as a benchmark at long lead times}

\begin{rc}
[\ldots] Fig.\ 3 and Fig.\ 4 show that 10-day and months ``persistence forecasts'' as
benchmark which is unrealistic in practice.
\end{rc}

Persistence can be competitive at short lead times. We now recommend matching the benchmark
to the decision horizon: persistence for hourly to two-day operations and climatology for
weekly to seasonal horizons. The captions state that persistence is retained beyond two days
only to show its degradation and is not a meaningful long-horizon reference.

The figure captions now carry the same qualification.

Adding a climatological benchmark would be interesting but a defensible daily climatology
for these locations requires a long record of calculated inflow at the dams. While there are
nearby USGS gages, these reflect older operations and so would not give a representative
climatology. For this reason we have not included it in the figures. 

\subsection*{1.3 Operating problems and the forecasting scheme in practice}

\begin{rc}
What are the main operation problems of these hydropower stations? Which forecasting
scheme is used in practice?
\end{rc}

We have added a subsection, ``Operating context.'' The three dams have limited storage relative to
their inflow and are operated as run-of-river facilities, with a daily cycle of an upward
ramp, flexible generation, a downward ramp, and refill. Generation is bid into the ISO New
England day-ahead market, so the day-ahead inflow volume is the quantity that matters most.
The binding constraint comes from the FERC relicensing of the three lower Connecticut River
dams, which imposed a $\pm$0.5 foot ($\approx$15~cm) limit on forebay fluctuation with
penalties for exceedance. The central operating problem is committing generation a day
ahead on an uncertain inflow forecast without violating the forebay limits, which is what
motivated the cumulative-error and threshold-exceedance metrics.

On the scheme used in practice: the operators used an in-house forecast built from National Weather
Service River Forecast Center forecasts, routed to the dam locations and adjusted using operator
judgment. This is the incumbent against which the two commercial forecasts were evaluated, labeled
GRH in the results, and it extends only about 32 hours because the day-ahead bid is the only
decision it is used for.

\subsection*{1.4 Description of the forecast products}

\begin{rc}
Two commercial forecasts (A, B) and the GRH in-house forecast (GRH) should be
described in detail, such as what type of hydrologic model used? How about the
calibration and validation results, what type input data requirement etc.
\end{rc}

Reviewers 2 and 4 raised the same point (comments 2.16 and 4.3). We agree that
anonymization without any methodological characterization leaves readers unable to
interpret the results. We have added a subsection describing what we are able to disclose:

\begin{newtext}
``Forecast A is produced by a consulting firm using a conceptual rainfall-runoff model with
hydraulic routing through the Connecticut River main stem, driven by a commercial numerical
weather prediction forecast, and issued once daily at an hourly time step out to roughly 8
days. Forecast B is produced by a technology firm using a machine learning model trained on
gauge and remotely sensed catchment observations, issued twice daily at an hourly time step out
to 10 days. The GRH in-house forecast combines RFC forecasts with routing and operator
judgment and extends only through the day-ahead period, roughly 32 hours, because that is
the horizon it is used for. Neither vendor released model internals or calibration and
validation diagnostics, and we did not have access to them. A utility evaluating commercial
forecast products generally cannot inspect them and must judge them on operational
performance, which is part of the motivation for this framework.''
\end{newtext}

The issue frequencies and horizons are taken from the forecast archive rather than vendor
documentation, so they describe what was actually delivered.

We cannot provide calibration and validation results for these models. The data used in this study was provided
on the basis that it be anonymized. The lack of detailed information about the models reflects
the reality that utilities face when procuring commercial forecasts. We have also added that a
full procurement evaluation should weigh ongoing operational costs, data dependencies, and ease
of integration alongside skill, since these may differ substantially between commercial
products.

\subsection*{1.6 NSE values in Table 1}

\begin{rc}
In Table 1, the NSE values of forecast scheme A at Wilder, Bellows and Vermon
watersheds are 0.72, 0.55, 0.81, respectively. Please check the evaluation results.

We recomputed the values; 0.72, 0.55, and 0.81 are correct. The accompanying text was not:
it called these values KGE and has been corrected.

Forecast A does not have smaller errors at Bellows Falls than at Wilder. Its RMSE is
5,904~cfs at Bellows Falls and 2,278~cfs at Wilder. The apparent discrepancy arises because
NSE is normalized by observed variance and should not be used by itself to rank accuracy
across sites. The persistence rows make the site dependence visible: persistence NSE is
$-0.81$ at Wilder, 0.39 at Bellows Falls, and 0.80 at Vernon.

We now compare products primarily within a location and interpret NSE together with RMSE,
sample coverage, and the benchmark rows. We also repeated the calculation over the 510 days
common to all products; the within-location product rankings do not change. An NSE skill
score considered during revision was removed because the product and reference samples were
not identical. A reference skill score is valid only when both forecasts use the same
observations.

\subsection*{2.1 Engagement with the forecast value literature}

\begin{rc}
The stated goal of the framework is to help operators evaluate whether a forecast
product will improve operations enough to justify investment. The paper does not
include any assessment of forecast value [\ldots] The authors do not need to perform a
full value assessment, but they should reference this literature, discuss how it
connects to their framework, and provide operators with at least a conceptual pathway
from the metrics presented to the investment decision the paper is motivated by.
\end{rc}

We now distinguish quality from value in the abstract, introduction, and discussion. Quality
describes forecasts relative to observations. Value also depends on the decision, its
consequences, and risk preferences. We cite Thompson and Brier (1955) for cost-loss utility,
Laugesen et al.\ (2023) for relative utility value, and Farkas et al.\ (2025) for a broader
review.

We also give a GRH-specific pathway. The quality evaluation first screens products and creates
an archive of forecasts as issued. Candidate forecasts can then drive a scheduling and dispatch
model containing reservoir constraints, unit performance curves, day-ahead and real-time
prices, and the incumbent operating strategy. Benefits should be measured relative to the
forecast and decisions currently used. The forebay metric is decision-relevant but remains a
stress test because it omits schedules and operator response.

\subsection*{2.2 Positioning of the contribution}

\begin{rc}
The five-step iterative process [\ldots] reads more as standard good operational practice
than a novel framework contribution. I feel that the tailored metrics and operator
feedback loop are where the genuine contribution lies, especially in the real-time
context, and these deserve more prominence and deeper treatment.
\end{rc}

We restructured the manuscript around this distinction. Please see our response to the editor
for the revised statement of contribution.

The specific changes: steps 1 through 3 are labeled as generic practice for which we claim no
novelty. Soliciting feedback and acting on it are separated into ``Gather feedback'' and ``Update
metrics,'' since collecting operator comment and changing the metric set are different activities
and the second is the one that matters, so the process is now six steps. The mechanics of both
have been more carefully documented, including a table tracing each tailored metric to the operator
concern that prompted it (3.2), and the two timescales conflated in the iteration step are
separated (3.3). We have added the case where a tailored metric separates two products that the
generic metrics do not (3.5), a discussion subsection comparing the framework against retrospective
evaluation (3.4), and the point that operational reliability is itself part of what a real-time
evaluation measures.

\subsection*{2.4 Verification improving alongside forecasts}

\begin{rc}
(L12--13) The sentences seem to imply that we should expect verification methods to

You are right on both counts and the implication was unintentional. The passage has been

\subsection*{2.6 The argument against hindcast evaluation}

\begin{rc}
(L45--47) The argument against hindcast evaluation is reasonable but somewhat
overstated. Hindcast evaluations do not necessarily require operators to re-operate
the system, especially if verifying forecasts of environmental variables such as
inflows, which you are.
\end{rc}

Agreed,  verifying an inflow forecast against
observed inflow does not require re-operating the system. The re-operation requirement applies to
the value assessment rather than the quality assessment, which is the distinction your first major
comment prompted us to draw. We have revised the text, and clarified the two complications:
where a forecast is modified by human judgment, a hindcast requires forecasters to reconstruct
decisions made under past conditions including electricity prices and weather. Extending the
evaluation from quality to value requires a reservoir operations model or manual operator input.

Additionally, the calculated inflows we
verify against are derived from forebay elevation changes and dam outflow, so the observation record
is itself a function of how the system was operated. This does not require re-operation to
interpret, but it does mean our reference data is not independent of operations in the way a gauge
record would be. We have added this to the limitations.

\subsection*{2.8 Smoothing window width}

\subsection*{2.10 The claim about KGE and large values}

\begin{rc}
(L92--93) The claim that KGE is not skewed by large values while NSE and RMSE are
requires clarification. KGE and NSE both use correlation as a component. The relevant
difference is that KGE decomposes performance into correlation, variability ratio, and
bias ratio rather than using squared error. The statement as written is misleading.
\end{rc}

You are right, and your characterization is the correct one. The original sentence attributed
the difference to the squared error term, but KGE also incorporates correlation, which is
itself computed from squared deviations, so the stated reason does not hold. We have replaced
it with:

\begin{newtext}
``We recommend KGE because it decomposes forecast performance into correlation, a variability
ratio, and a bias ratio, so that a low score can be attributed to a specific deficiency
rather than only registered as large aggregate error. NSE and RMSE aggregate squared error
into a single number, which makes them more strongly influenced by the largest errors and
less diagnostic about their source.''
\end{newtext}

We also state that KGE, NSE, RMSE, bias, and correlation answer different questions and should
be interpreted together with sample coverage and benchmark forecasts.

\subsection*{2.18 Seasonal performance}

\begin{rc}
(L134) ``Forecasts tend to perform worse in the winter which is the wet season and worse
in the summer which is the dry season.'' This says forecasts perform worse in both wet and
dry seasons. Please clarify when forecasts actually perform well and why.
\end{rc}

You are correct, this was mixing up streamflow vs precipitation. 
Mean inflow at Vernon is highest in March and April and lowest from
September through November, so the high-flow season is spring, driven by snowmelt, and that is
where skill is highest for all three products.

The rewritten passage states this, and explains the mechanism: performance is poorest in the
seasons where inflow is driven by precipitation events, rain and rain-on-snow in winter and
convective storms in summer, both of which depend on highly uncertain precipitation forecasts,
whereas snowmelt-driven inflow is comparatively predictable because it is governed by an
accumulated snowpack observable in advance that melts over weeks, so skill depends more on
temperature, which is forecast more reliably.

Two further corrections came out of this. The original text gave the snowmelt season as April
through July, which does not match the monthly inflow pattern; it is now March through May. And
the January and February KGE values for forecast A were given as 0.39 and $-0.40$; recomputing
gives 0.45 and $-0.67$. The revised value is also consistent with the figure, in which the
February point falls below the plotted range, which $-0.40$ would not.

\subsection*{2.19 The inflow-equals-outflow assumption}

\begin{rc}
(L146) [\ldots] the forebay exceedance analysis assumes an inflow=outflow condition which
is operationally unrealistic as the authors note. It would strengthen the analysis to
discuss how operators actually deviate from this assumption and what the real-world
exceedance rates might be.
\end{rc}

Operators do not hold pass-inflow conditions. They schedule generation to a market bid, depart
from inflow during the day, recover the forebay in off-peak hours, and intervene to keep the forebay 
in the operating band. We now call the calculation an idealized stress test and readily acknowledge that
the system would never be operated in this way. It is neither a prediction nor a bound on
realized compliance rates because operator actions can increase or decrease the modeled risk. 
Estimating realized rates requires schedules, constraints, and a model of operator response, as
described under comment 4.9.

\subsection*{2.20 Extremes and the record length}

\begin{rc}
(L178--182) [\ldots] Consider also noting the limitation that almost two years of data may
not capture the full range of extreme events which are often the conditions where forecast
quality matters most for operational decisions.
\end{rc}

Agreed, and we have stated the limitation explicitly. Reviewer 4 asked in comment 4.2 what the
period did contain, so we addressed both together; please see that response for the events
observed and their position in the long-term annual peak record. In short, the record covers a
useful range but not the upper tail, and no severe multi-year drought.

One point specific to this system is worth adding: representing multiple high flow events
matters less at GRH than it might elsewhere, because operational flexibility is limited during
high flows and operators discount forecast errors in those conditions. The gap that does matter
for GRH is the absence of a strong multi-year drought, which we state as a limitation.

\subsection*{3.1 ``market prices''}

\begin{rc}

We mean day-ahead and real-time wholesale electricity prices, which affect generation bids and
release schedules. Historical prices can often be retrieved. The harder quantities to
reconstruct are outages, operating constraints, operator judgment, and how price signals changed
decisions. We corrected this overstatement and now apply the reconstruction problem to a value

\subsection*{3.2 How feedback is reflected in the process}

\begin{rc}
(\S2.1) Step 1 through 3 are so general. I assume that steps 4 and 5 are unique points in
this manuscript. Please provide a more detailed explanation of how feedback is reflected in
step 4.
\end{rc}

You have identified the structure of the contribution correctly, and we now say so in the text
rather than presenting all steps as equally novel. Steps 1 through 3 are labeled as generic.

Your comment also exposed an ambiguity: what was a single step for soliciting feedback conflated
gathering operator comment with actually changing the metric set. We have split them into
``Gather feedback'' and ``Update metrics,'' so the process is now six steps, with 4 through 6
being where it departs from retrospective verification.

Both steps are expanded with the mechanics. Feedback was collected in recurring meetings, monthly
for most of the evaluation, each reviewing the current metrics against recent operating experience,
with operators asked which recent forecast errors mattered operationally and which did not. Their
answers frequently diverged from what the generic metrics indicated, especially in the high-flow
season: they discounted large errors during spill conditions, when they had little discretion over
releases and forecast quality was operationally irrelevant, while treating moderate errors with the
potential to breach the forebay compliance band as serious. That is what motivated the tailored
metrics, the exceedance metrics in particular.

We have also added a table to the manuscript tracing each tailored metric to the operator concern
that prompted it and the approximate month it was added, so the process is auditable rather than
asserted: day-ahead volumetric skill, because market bids are set from next-day volume rather than
hourly shape (months 1--2); the seasonal breakdown, because products were suspected to behave
differently in snowmelt and rain driven regimes (3--4); cumulative forebay error, because average
error does not indicate whether the band would be breached (4--6); and forebay exceedance
probability, because operators needed to know how quickly cumulative error could exhaust the
operating band (5--6).

We should be candid that the months are approximate: the evaluation predates this paper and we
kept no formal log of metric provenance, so these are reconstructed from meeting materials and
correspondence, though the sequence and motivations are accurate. A practical recommendation
follows, which we now state: an evaluation of this kind should log metric provenance as it goes,
since the record of why each metric exists is part of what makes the results defensible later.

\subsection*{3.3 Is a one-year learning period required?}

\begin{rc}
(``5. Iterate'') This implies that at least one year learning period is required. Is this
understanding correct?
\end{rc}

The appropriate duration depends on the system. In this case metric development was complete
within roughly six months, after which the evaluation accumulated data. Because the products
show strong seasonal variation, we recommend at least one annual cycle for this application.
That recommendation is not a universal minimum.

Our own results bear on the second point. Pooled day-ahead KGE for forecast A ranges from
$-0.67$ in February to 0.88 in May, so an evaluation conducted in spring alone would have reached
a substantially more favorable conclusion about that product than the full record supports. We
have also noted the tension the operators raised, that industry preference runs toward shorter
evaluations, sometimes a single season, while a single season samples only one hydrologic
outcome.

\subsection*{3.4 Comparison with traditional methods}

\begin{rc}
(result of case study) Were all the results derived through the proposed framework? If so,
please compare those result with those obtained via ``traditional'' methods.
\end{rc}

All results were produced through the framework, and we agree the comparison you describe is what
demonstrates its effectiveness. We have added it in two forms.

First, the new ``Comparison with retrospective forecast evaluation'' subsection states that a
hindcast provides larger samples, more extremes, and broader hydrologic coverage. Historical
wholesale prices may be available, but outages, operating constraints, operator judgment, and
the response to market signals may not be recoverable. A real-time evaluation records these
conditions as they occur. It complements rather than replaces a hindcast.

Second, the comparison of conclusions. A conventional verification of these forecasts, computing
NSE, KGE, PBIAS, and correlation over the full record, concludes that forecast B outperforms
forecast A and is comparable to the in-house forecast. That conclusion is correct and this
framework reproduces it. What the conventional presentation omits is the horizon at which
cumulative forecast error can exhaust the forebay band (3.5). It also requires care when
comparing efficiency metrics across locations because their values depend on observed variance
(1.6). The approaches agree on the overall product ranking; the tailored metric expresses that
ranking in units tied to a GRH constraint.

\subsection*{3.5 Advantage of tailored metrics}

\begin{rc}
(\S3.1 Tailored metrics) Please explain the advantage of introducing tailored metrics. For
example, it would be helpful to show a case where using tailored metrics led to a different
forecast onclusion compared to general metrics.
\end{rc}

The generic and tailored metrics do not select different products: forecast B outperforms A on
both. The tailored metric adds the decision horizon. Under the idealized neutral-start stress
test, exceedance probabilities over the first 24 hours of each product's horizon are lower for B
than for A at all three dams: 31.0\% versus 56.6\% at Wilder, 47.7\% versus 67.9\% at Bellows
Falls, and 53.8\% versus 71.1\% at Vernon. By 32 hours, the longest horizon the three products
share, B reaches 42.8--65.5\% while A reaches 61.2--82.0\%. Product B therefore holds a
consistent advantage, but passive use of any of the products is untenable: by the end of the
first day each leaves the band in roughly a third to three-quarters of issues. NSE, KGE, PBIAS,
and correlation do not express this result in forebay units.

On comparison to past studies, the introduction already discussed the head-to-head evaluation
exercises (the HEPEX community experiments and the Bureau of Reclamation forecast rodeos), and we have
sharpened what distinguishes this work: those evaluate forecast quality in general, while this
framework evaluates fitness for one utility's operating constraints under live conditions.

\subsection*{4.1 Smoothing window size and sensitivity}

\begin{rc}
Lines 80--81 recommend a triangular smoother for calculated inflows but do not specify the
smoothing window size [\ldots] The window size must be stated explicitly, and a sensitivity
analysis or justification for the chosen value would strengthen the paper.

The revised methods state the filter and report a sensitivity analysis. We apply two successive
24-hour moving averages to hourly calculated inflow. Their convolution produces a triangular
kernel spanning 47 observations. The 0, 6, 12, and 24-hour labels below refer to the width of
each component moving average. We selected 24 hours to match the day-ahead volumetric decision.
Day-ahead NSE over the four component widths is:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llrrrr}
\toprule
Location & Forecast & 0 h & 6 h & 12 h & 24 h \\
\midrule
Wilder        & A   & 0.70 & 0.72 & 0.72 & 0.72 \\
              & B   & 0.82 & 0.83 & 0.83 & 0.83 \\
              & GRH & 0.87 & 0.87 & 0.88 & 0.88 \\
Bellows Falls & A   & 0.50 & 0.51 & 0.52 & 0.55 \\
              & B   & 0.90 & 0.90 & 0.90 & 0.91 \\
              & GRH & 0.84 & 0.83 & 0.84 & 0.85 \\
Vernon        & A   & 0.79 & 0.79 & 0.79 & 0.81 \\
              & B   & 0.88 & 0.88 & 0.88 & 0.89 \\
              & GRH & 0.88 & 0.88 & 0.88 & 0.89 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Metrics improve modestly with window width, as expected since smoothing removes observation noise
that no forecast can reproduce; the largest change is 0.05 NSE (Bellows Falls, forecast A). The
improvement is not strictly monotonic, the in-house forecast at Bellows Falls dipping from 0.84 to
0.83 between 0 and 6 hours before recovering, which we attribute to sampling rather than to any

smoothing and by 0.37 with 24-hour smoothing. The 24-hour column reproduces Table 1 exactly, so the
sensitivity analysis and the main results come from the same pipeline.

The revised text recommends reporting the filter, testing sensitivity to its width, and matching
the component window to the decision timescale.

\subsection*{4.4 Extension to probabilistic and ensemble forecasts}

\begin{rc}
The paper focuses only on deterministic forecast evaluation [\ldots] the framework could be
briefly extended to probabilistic or ensemble forecasts using metrics such as CRPS, reliability
diagrams, or rank histograms.
\end{rc}

We agree and have added a methods subsection, ``Extension to probabilistic and ensemble forecasts.''
Reviewer 2 asked for the same in comment 2.5. It makes the point that the framework is agnostic to
whether the forecast is deterministic or probabilistic, since only the metrics and visualizations
change and not the process, and notes that CRPS is the natural accuracy measure for ensembles, with
the useful property that it reduces to mean absolute error for a deterministic forecast so the two
can be compared on a common scale, alongside rank histograms and reliability diagrams for assessing
ensemble properties.

Two connections are worth drawing out. The forebay exceedance metric we develop is already a
probabilistic statement derived from a deterministic forecast, computed across the empirical
distribution of that forecast's past errors; given an ensemble it would be computed directly from the
members, which is both simpler and better founded. And operators bidding into a day-ahead market must
submit a single quantity, so a probabilistic forecast must be reduced to a point value at the moment
of the bid, which is why improvements in deterministic skill remain directly useful. This is a
description of how the framework would extend rather than a demonstration, since all three products
evaluated here are deterministic.

\subsection*{4.9 What coupling to scheduling models would entail}

On the data required, such a coupling would route each candidate forecast through a scheduling model to

requirement is a further reason a real-time evaluation is a useful precursor, since it produces exactly
this archive as a byproduct. The same coupling is what a full value assessment (2.1) and an estimate of
realized forebay exceedance rates (2.19) would both require.
