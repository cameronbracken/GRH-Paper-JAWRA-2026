\subsection{Smoothing calculated inflows}
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Most available reservoir inflow data is not directly measured, instead it is computed based on measured }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Reservoir inflow is often calculated from }\DIFaddend changes in forebay elevation and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{a known }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{an }\DIFaddend elevation-volume relationship \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. These calculations are often noisy due to short term fluctuations in forebay elevations from }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{rather than measured directly. Short-term changes caused by }\DIFaddend wind, turbine and spillway \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{release changes, and other such as reservoir seichesor waves propagating from upstream . Inflow computed in this way can often contain negative values and other non-physical fluctuations. In this situation we recommend the inflow data be smoothed prior to comparing with any forecasted }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{releases, seiches, and upstream waves can produce noise and nonphysical values. \subsection{Traditional inflow evaluation metrics}

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Traditional deterministic forecast evaluation metrics that are particularly relevant to inflow forecasting include }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Deterministic inflow forecasts can be evaluated using }\DIFaddend measures of average \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{behavior}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{error}\DIFaddend , correlation, and bias. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Additional metrics include higher order momentsof the forecast distributions, categorical forecast }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Higher moments, categorical }\DIFaddend performance, and conditional statistics \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{st-aubinPrecisionReliabilityForecasts2022, hewamalageForecastEvaluationData2023}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. This section is not intended to be a comprehensive review of deterministic forecast evaluation metrics . Therefore, we recommend some simple metrics which can be augmented as needed for particular applications. These metrics should be computed for a variety of lead times as well as seasonally to fully assess forecast performanceacross a range of conditions}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{may also be useful \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{st-aubinPrecisionReliabilityForecasts2022,hewamalageForecastEvaluationData2023}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. We use a small set of metrics that can be expanded for a particular application. Each should be calculated by lead time and season because aggregate values can hide changes in performance}\DIFaddend .

Average hydrologic forecast behavior is typically measured with an error metric such as root mean squared error (RMSE), Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, or King-Gupta }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{nashRiverFlowForecasting1970}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, or Kling-Gupta }\DIFaddend Efficiency (KGE) \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{(refs)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{guptaDecompositionMeanSquared2009}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFaddend . We recommend KGE because it \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is not skewed by large values where }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{decomposes forecast performance into correlation, a variability ratio, and a bias ratio, so that a low score can be attributed to a specific deficiency rather than only registered as large aggregate error. }\DIFaddend NSE and RMSE \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{are due to the use of a squared error term}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{aggregate squared error into a single number, which makes them more strongly influenced by the largest errors and less diagnostic about their source}\DIFaddend . The KGE is given by

\begin{equation}
    \text{KGE}=1-\sqrt{(r-1)^2+\left(\frac{\sigma_f}{\sigma_o}-1\right)^2+\left(\frac{\mu_f}{\mu_o}-1\right)^2}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the linear correlation coefficient, $\sigma_f$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is the standard deviation of the forecast, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and }\DIFaddend $\sigma_o$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is the standard deviation of the observations, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{are forecast and observed standard deviations, and }\DIFaddend $\mu_f$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is the mean of the forecast, }\DIFdelend and $\mu_o$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is the mean of the observations. Values of KGE that are greater than -0.41 are better than the mean of the observations, but larger values are preferred up to the maximum value of 1 for a perfect forecast. We note that }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{are forecast and observed means. KGE values greater than $-0.41$ improve on the observed mean as a predictor \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{knobenTechnicalNoteInherent2019a}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
; the maximum is 1. }\DIFaddend NSE and KGE have different properties and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{so }\DIFdelend should not be used interchangeably\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{knobenTechnicalNoteInherent2019a}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{. We report both and use RMSE, bias, correlation, and benchmark forecasts to interpret them.
}\DIFaddend

The \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{bias }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{error, or residual, }\DIFaddend of a single forecast \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{point }\DIFdelend is the difference between \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{observed and forecasted values but this may be expressed as a percentage }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the forecast and the observation. Bias is the systematic component of error, the tendency of a forecast to be too high or too low on average, and is conventionally expressed }\DIFaddend for a set of forecasts \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{by averaging across all bias values
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{as a percentage of total observed volume
}\DIFaddend

\begin{equation}
    \text{PBIAS} = 100\ [\%]\times\frac{\sum(f_i-o_i)}{\sum(o_i)}
\end{equation}

where \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\text{PBIAS}$ measures the tendency of a forecast ($f$) to be greater than (positive values ) or less than (negative values ) than observations($o$)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{positive values indicate forecasts greater than observations and negative values indicate forecasts less than observations}\DIFaddend .

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Correlation measures the strength of a linear relationship }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Linear association }\DIFaddend between observations and forecasts is \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{computed }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{measured }\DIFaddend using the Pearson correlation coefficient

\begin{equation}
    r = \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\frac{\sum(o_i-\mu_o)(f_i-\mu_f)}{\sqrt{\sum(o_i-\mu_o)^2(f_i-\mu_f)^2}}
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\frac{\sum(o_i-\mu_o)(f_i-\mu_f)}{\sqrt{\sum(o_i-\mu_o)^2\ \sum(f_i-\mu_f)^2}}
}\DIFaddend \end{equation}

where $r$ can range from -1 to 1 but for forecast evaluation, negative values would indicate poor forecast performance.

\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Metrics should be computed on a common sample when products cover different periods. Skill scores relative to a reference forecast are also useful, but the evaluated and reference forecasts must use the same observations. Otherwise differences in sample variance can be mistaken for differences in skill.
}

\subsection{\DIFadd{Extension to probabilistic and ensemble forecasts}}\label{sec:probabilistic}

\DIFadd{The process applies to deterministic and probabilistic forecasts; the metrics and figures change. Probabilistic products can describe risk around compliance or market decisions. The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), rank histograms, and reliability diagrams are common choices. CRPS reduces to mean absolute error for a deterministic forecast, which permits comparison on a common scale.
}

\subsection{\DIFadd{Operating context}}\label{sec:context}

\DIFadd{The three dams have limited storage relative to their inflow and are operated as run-of-river facilities. Their daily cycle consists of an upward ramp, a period of flexible generation, a downward ramp, and a refill period. Generation is bid into the ISO New England day-ahead market, so the day-ahead inflow volume is the quantity that matters most to the operators, since it sets what they can bid into the market. The binding operational constraint comes from the FERC relicensing of the three lower Connecticut River dams, which imposed a $\pm$0.5 foot ($\approx$15 cm) limit on forebay fluctuation, with penalties for exceedance. The central operating problem is therefore committing generation based on an uncertain inflow forecast without violating the forebay limits. This is what motivated the cumulative-error and threshold-exceedance metrics in Section \ref{sec:tailored}.
}

\DIFadd{At the time of the evaluation the operators used an in-house forecast built from National Weather Service River Forecast Center forecasts, routed to the dam locations and adjusted using operator judgment. This is the incumbent product against which the two commercial forecasts were evaluated, and it is labeled GRH in the results.
}

\section{Discussion}\DIFaddbegin \label{sec:discussion}
\DIFaddend %i)	Recommendation
%ii)	Value of the process
%iii)	Limitations

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{In this paper we present a general framework that can be used in real time (or near real time) for reservoir inflow forecast evaluation. The evaluation can include forecast verification and validation but also include other qualitative or specialized metrics of forecast quality. The framework calls for an iterative approach which involves direct operator feedback in the process. We also presented a successful application of this evaluation framework to three reservoirs in the Great River Hydro system on the Connecticut River in the Northeast U. S. While there is no single evaluation approach that can apply to all forecasts, we present a range of options of both traditional and tailored metrics that will be suitable for many situations. The goal of such a framework is to provide operators with information necessary for evaluating how much a particular forecast product will improve their operations and ultimately inform ongoing investment decisions (i.e. purchase external forecast products or improve existing internal forecast). }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{We presented a procedure for comparing reservoir inflow forecasts under current operating conditions when hindcasts are unavailable. It combines standard verification with metrics developed from operator concerns. At GRH, product B generally matched the in-house forecast and outperformed A. The forebay stress test identified a day-ahead advantage for B that aggregate metrics did not express in operational terms. The procedure supports product screening, but it does not by itself estimate revenue or compliance outcomes.
}\DIFaddend

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{In our real time evaluationof the GRH inflow forecasts there was one commercial forecast that stood out as having better performance overall. However, it was not always the best }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \subsection{\DIFadd{Comparison with retrospective forecast evaluation}}

\DIFadd{A retrospective evaluation can provide larger samples, more extremes, and broader hydrologic coverage than the less than two years available here. Forecast quality can be evaluated directly from hindcasts. Reconstructing value is more difficult. Historical wholesale prices may be available, but outages, constraints, operator judgment, and the response to market conditions may not be. A real-time evaluation observes these conditions as they occur, but it cannot replace a hindcast where one is feasible. The two approaches answer complementary questions.
}


\subsection{\DIFadd{From forecast quality to forecast value}}

\DIFadd{The metrics presented here measure forecast quality, not forecast value. Quality describes the relation between forecasts and observations. Value also depends on the decision, its consequences, and the decision maker's risk preferences. A more accurate forecast has no added value when it does not change an action. Classical cost-loss analysis links forecast use to decision utility \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{thompsonEconomicUtilityWeather1955}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. The relative utility value framework extends this logic to binary, multicategory, and continuous decisions and can represent risk aversion \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{laugesenFlexibleForecastValue2023}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citet{farkasEconomicValueWeather2025} }\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
review related approaches.
}

\DIFadd{The tailored metrics move toward value by expressing error in units tied to a decision. The forebay metric is still a stress test, not a valuation, because it omits actual schedules and operator responses. A GRH value study would first screen products using the quality evaluation, then route the archived forecasts through a scheduling and dispatch model containing reservoir constraints, unit performance curves, day-ahead and real-time prices, and the incumbent operating strategy. Benefits should be measured relative to the forecast and decisions currently used, with sensitivity tests for risk preferences and alternative operating strategies.
}

\subsection{\DIFadd{Limitations and next steps}}

\DIFadd{Forecast B performed better overall, but it did not lead }\DIFaddend in every month \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{and every lead time. This underscores the difficulty of selecting a single best deterministic forecast. Moving to a probabilistic forecast may alleviate this somewhat but operators still need to bid in a fixed amount into energy markets so overall improvements in deterministic forecast skill are valuable. In this study we did not address how much improvements in inflow forecasts will translate into improvements in hydropower operations and increased revenue. An important next step in this evaluation framework is to couple this evaluation with hydropower scheduling models to quantify increases in revenue from improved inflow forecasts . While this seems a straight forward next steps, many hydropower scheduling models work with ranges of operations that match the utility's bidding strategy and local market opportunities. This complexity implies that evaluating the opportunities for harnessing higher forecast accuracy may also requires updating scheduling models and exploring more monetized operations}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{or at every horizon. Product selection should therefore consider the decisions and seasons that matter most. Probabilistic forecasts can describe uncertainty, although operators must still submit fixed market bids.
}

\DIFadd{A second lesson concerns comparisons among locations. Raw efficiency metrics depend on observed variability, so they should not be used alone to rank sites. RMSE, sample coverage, and reference forecasts provide necessary context. Comparisons among products should use the same dates whenever coverage differs.
}

\DIFadd{We did not estimate how forecast improvements affect operations or revenue. That analysis would couple the archived forecasts to a hydropower scheduling model. A model configured to reproduce the current bidding strategy may understate the benefit of a more accurate forecast because the strategy was developed around the incumbent product. A value study may therefore need to revise the scheduling model and operating strategy along with the forecast input}\DIFaddend .

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Real time forecast evaluations are inherently limited by the amount of time they are conducted. Ideally a forecast model's performance would be assessed over the full range of hydrologic conditionsspanning multiple decades. A hindcast evaluation may account for the full range of hydrologic variability but this requires an operations model or considerable effort on the part of the system operators to re-operate the system for every hindcast. The decision to pursue a real time or hindcast evaluation must be made by the system operators depending on their specific system needs and resource availability}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The record length limits the real-time evaluation. The period included a regional flood and a rain-on-snow event (Section \ref{sec:period}), but no multi-year drought. We could not assess product performance under sustained water-limited conditions}\DIFaddend . \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{A multi-decade hindcast would cover more hydrologic regimes.
}\DIFaddend

\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The choice between real-time and retrospective evaluation depends on the system and available resources. We recommend both when feasible. For this seasonally varying system, at least one year of real-time evaluation is appropriate. A shorter period samples only part of the annual cycle, so its conclusions are conditional on the seasons and events observed.
}

\DIFaddend The \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{iterative nature of this framework is perhaps its most valuable component. Having direct operator involvement and feedback helps to provide value to the operators. The development of tailored metrics make the results relevant to a particular system and helps with operator buy-in. Furthermore, both generic and tailored metrics can ultimately be extended to assess the impacts of inflow forecasts on hydropower scheduling.
Particularly under dynamic electricity market conditions, evaluations of inflow forecasts can serve as key indicators for understanding how forecasting errors propagate through the system, influencing compliance with operational requirements, variations in actual power generation, and potential revenue for system operators. The analysis of inflow forecast performance, conducted in coordination with system operators, represents an important foundational step toward achieving these broader applications. In presenting this framework we hope to provide a template for further applications and unbiased evaluation of the myriad of inflow forecasts available to hydropower system operators}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{reference inflows are calculated from forebay elevation changes and dam outflow. The observations therefore depend on operations and are not independent in the way a gauge record would be. The three products also cover different portions of the evaluation period (Section \ref{sec:context}), so Table \ref{tab:metrics} uses overlapping but nonidentical samples. Restricting the analysis to common dates does not change the ranking. We report the full records to retain the available data.
}

Together they provide the forecast archive and quality screen needed for a later scheduling and value analysis}\DIFaddend .
