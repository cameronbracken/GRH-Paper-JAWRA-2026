\DIFdelbegin %DIFDELCMD < \abstract[Abstract]{
%DIFDELCMD < Recent advances in data driven hydrologic forecasts have led to a multitude of new and rapidly improving streamflow forecast products and technologies. These often lead hydropower producers to question whether such advances translate into increases in revenue and whether implementing these new forecasts justify the additional associated costs. To address this, we present a general framework that can be used in real time (or near real time) for reservoir inflow forecast evaluation. The framework calls for an iterative approach which involves direct operator feedback in the evaluation process. The evaluation can include forecast verification and validation, but also includes other qualitative or specialized metrics of forecast quality. We also present a successful application of this evaluation framework to three reservoirs in the Great River Hydro system on the Connecticut River in the Northeast U.S. While there is no single evaluation approach that can apply to all hydrologic forecasts, we also present a range of options of both traditional and tailored metrics that will be suitable for a diverse set of situations and systems. \DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \abstract[Abstract]{
Recent advances in hydrologic forecasting have produced a growing number of streamflow forecast products. Hydropower operators must determine whether these products improve operations enough to justify their cost. We present a framework for comparing multiple reservoir inflow forecasts over a common period under current operating conditions. This design is useful when product-specific hindcasts cover different periods, model versions, or system configurations. Operators define the decisions and constraints to be evaluated, participate in selecting and revising metrics, and interpret results using the same observations and operating context for every product. We apply the framework to three reservoirs in the Great River Hydro system on the Connecticut River in the Northeast U.S. The commercial product labeled B generally matched the in-house forecast and outperformed product A. Under an idealized cumulative-error stress test, product B also had a lower probability of exceeding the forebay compliance band during the first 24 hours at all three dams. Its advantage persisted through the 32-hour horizon shared by all products, although exceedance probabilities were high enough that none of the forecasts could be used without operator intervention. The real-time evaluation screens products and defines the constraints for a subsequent hindcast-based value assessment using a scheduling model, operating constraints, and market prices.
}

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Most available reservoir inflow data is not directly measured, instead it is computed based on measured }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Reservoir inflow is often calculated from }\DIFaddend changes in forebay elevation and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{a known }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{an }\DIFaddend elevation-volume relationship \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. Short-term changes caused by }\DIFaddend wind, turbine and spillway \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{release changes, and other such as reservoir seichesor waves propagating from upstream . Inflow computed in this way can often contain negative values and other non-physical fluctuations. In this situation we recommend the inflow data be smoothed prior to comparing with any forecasted inflow . We recommend a triangular smoother that applies moving-averages using a triangular weight function. This is a simple option which produces reasonable results but many more options }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{releases, seiches, and waves from upstream can introduce noise and nonphysical values into the calculated inflow. Smoothing may therefore be appropriate before calculated and forecast inflow are compared. We used two successive moving averages, which produce a triangular weighting function, but other filters }\DIFaddend are available \citep{elshorbagyNoiseReductionChaotic2002}. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Figure \ref{fig:smoothing} shows an example of hourly computed inflow data which has been smoothed using a triangular smoother. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The smoothing window should correspond to the timescale of the decision. A window that is too short will retain noise, while one that is too long may remove variability that the forecast should reproduce. Because smoothing changes the quantity being evaluated, the filter should be reported and the sensitivity of the results to its width should be tested.
}\DIFaddend

\subsection{Traditional inflow evaluation metrics}

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Traditional deterministic forecast evaluation metrics that are particularly relevant to inflow forecasting include }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Deterministic inflow forecasts can be evaluated using }\DIFaddend measures of average \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{behavior}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{error}\DIFaddend , correlation, and bias. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Additional metrics include higher order momentsof the forecast distributions, categorical forecast }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Higher moments, categorical }\DIFaddend performance, and conditional statistics \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{st-aubinPrecisionReliabilityForecasts2022, hewamalageForecastEvaluationData2023}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. This section is not intended to be a comprehensive }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{may also be useful \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{st-aubinPrecisionReliabilityForecasts2022,hewamalageForecastEvaluationData2023}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. We do not attempt a complete }\DIFaddend review of deterministic forecast \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{evaluation metrics. Therefore, we recommend some simple metrics which can be augmented as needed for particular applications. These metrics should be computed for a variety of lead times as well as seasonally to fully assess forecast performance across a range of conditions}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{metrics}\DIFaddend . \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Instead, we use a small set that describes several aspects of performance and can be expanded for a particular application. No single metric fully describes forecast quality. The metrics should also be calculated by lead time and season because values aggregated across the full record can hide changes that matter to operations.
}\DIFaddend 

Average hydrologic forecast behavior is typically measured with an error metric such as root mean squared error (RMSE), Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, or King-Gupta }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{nashRiverFlowForecasting1970}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, or Kling-Gupta }\DIFaddend Efficiency (KGE) \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{(refs)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{guptaDecompositionMeanSquared2009}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFaddend . We recommend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{including }\DIFaddend KGE because it \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is not skewed by large values where }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{decomposes forecast performance into correlation, a variability ratio, and a bias ratio. A low score can therefore be associated with a specific deficiency rather than registered only as a large aggregate error. }\DIFaddend NSE and RMSE \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{are due to the use of a squared error term}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{aggregate squared error into a single number, which makes them more sensitive to the largest errors and less diagnostic about their source. The metrics answer different questions, so we report them together rather than use one as a complete measure of forecast quality}\DIFaddend . The KGE is given by

\begin{equation}
    \text{KGE}=1-\sqrt{(r-1)^2+\left(\frac{\sigma_f}{\sigma_o}-1\right)^2+\left(\frac{\mu_f}{\mu_o}-1\right)^2}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the linear correlation coefficient, $\sigma_f$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is the standard deviation of the forecast, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and }\DIFaddend $\sigma_o$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is the standard deviation of the observations, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{are the forecast and observed standard deviations, and }\DIFaddend $\mu_f$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is the mean of the forecast, }\DIFdelend and $\mu_o$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{is the mean of the observations. Values of KGE that are greater than -0.41 are better than the mean of the observations, but larger values are preferred up to }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{are the forecast and observed means. KGE values greater than $-0.41$ improve on the observed mean as a predictor \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{knobenTechnicalNoteInherent2019a}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, and }\DIFaddend the maximum value \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{of }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{is }\DIFaddend 1 for a perfect forecast. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{We note that NSE and KGE have different properties and so }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Because the corresponding reference value for NSE is 0, the two efficiency metrics }\DIFaddend should not be \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{used interchangeably \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{knobenTechnicalNoteInherent2019a}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{interpreted on the same scale. We report both and use RMSE, bias, correlation, and benchmark forecasts to interpret their values.
}\DIFaddend 

The \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{bias }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{error, or residual, }\DIFaddend of a single forecast \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{point }\DIFdelend is the difference between \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{observed and forecasted values but this may be expressed as a percentage }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the forecast and the observation. Bias is the systematic component of error, the tendency of a forecast to be too high or too low on average, and is conventionally expressed }\DIFaddend for a set of forecasts \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{by averaging across all bias values 
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{as a percentage of total observed volume
}\DIFaddend 

\begin{equation}
    \text{PBIAS} = 100\ [\%]\times\frac{\sum(f_i-o_i)}{\sum(o_i)}
\end{equation}

where \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\text{PBIAS}$ measures the tendency of a forecast ($f$) to be greater than (positive values ) or less than (negative values ) than observations($o$)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{positive values indicate forecasts greater than observations and negative values indicate forecasts less than observations}\DIFaddend .

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Correlation measures the strength of a linear relationship }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Linear association }\DIFaddend between observations and forecasts is \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{computed }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{measured }\DIFaddend using the Pearson correlation coefficient

\begin{equation}
    r = \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\frac{\sum(o_i-\mu_o)(f_i-\mu_f)}{\sqrt{\sum(o_i-\mu_o)^2(f_i-\mu_f)^2}}
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\frac{\sum(o_i-\mu_o)(f_i-\mu_f)}{\sqrt{\sum(o_i-\mu_o)^2\ \sum(f_i-\mu_f)^2}}
}\DIFaddend \end{equation}

where $r$ can range from -1 to 1 but for forecast evaluation, negative values would indicate poor forecast performance.

\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Metrics should be computed on a common sample when products cover different periods. Skill scores relative to a reference forecast are also useful, but the evaluated and reference forecasts must use the same observations. Otherwise differences in sample variance can be mistaken for differences in skill.
}

\subsection{\DIFadd{Extension to probabilistic and ensemble forecasts}}\label{sec:probabilistic}

\DIFadd{The process described in this section applies to both deterministic and probabilistic forecasts; only the metrics and visualizations change. Probabilistic products can describe uncertainty around compliance or market decisions rather than provide only a single expected value. Where ensemble or probabilistic products are available, the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) can summarize the accuracy of the full predictive distribution. CRPS reduces to mean absolute error for a deterministic forecast, which permits comparison on a common scale. Rank histograms can indicate whether an ensemble is biased or has too little or too much spread, while reliability diagrams compare forecast probabilities with observed frequencies. The choice among these metrics should follow the same process used for deterministic forecasts and reflect the decisions being evaluated.
}

\subsection{\DIFadd{Operating context}}\label{sec:context}

\DIFadd{The three dams have limited storage relative to their inflow and are operated as run-of-river facilities. Their daily cycle consists of an upward ramp, a period of flexible generation, a downward ramp, and a refill period. Generation is bid into the Independent System Operator (ISO) New England day-ahead market, so the day-ahead inflow volume is the quantity that matters most to the operators, since it sets what they can bid into the market. The binding operational constraint comes from the FERC relicensing of the three lower Connecticut River dams, which imposed a $\pm$0.5 foot ($\approx$15 cm) limit on forebay fluctuation, with penalties for exceedance. The central operating problem is therefore committing generation based on an uncertain inflow forecast without violating the forebay limits. This is what motivated the cumulative-error and threshold-exceedance metrics in Section \ref{sec:tailored}.
}

\DIFadd{At the time of the evaluation the operators used an in-house forecast built from National Weather Service River Forecast Center forecasts, routed to the dam locations and adjusted using operator judgment. }

\section{Discussion}\DIFaddbegin \label{sec:discussion}
\DIFaddend %i)	Recommendation
%ii)	Value of the process
%iii)	Limitations

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{In this paper we present a general framework that can be used in real time (or near real time) for reservoir inflow forecast evaluation. The  evaluation can include forecast verification and validation but also include other qualitative or specialized metricsof forecast quality. The framework calls for an iterative approach which involves direct operator feedback in the process. We also presented a successful application of this evaluation framework }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{We present a framework for comparing multiple reservoir inflow forecasts over the same period and under current operating conditions. Its contribution is the design of the operating trial: define the decisions and constraints before selecting metrics, align the product records, review results with operators at regular intervals, preserve why metrics change, and continue long enough to sample the relevant operating regimes. We applied this process }\DIFaddend to three reservoirs in the Great River Hydro system\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{on the Connecticut River in the Northeast U. S.
While there is no single evaluation approach that can apply to all forecasts, we present a range of options of both traditional and tailored metrics that will be suitable for many situations. The goal of such a framework is to provide operators with information necessary for evaluating how much a particular forecast product will improve their operations and ultimately inform ongoing investment decisions (i.e. purchase external forecast products or improve existing internal forecast ). }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{. Product B generally matched the in-house forecast and outperformed product A. The forebay stress test expressed this difference in operational units and showed how risk accumulated over the shared forecast horizon. These results screen forecast products and define requirements for a later value analysis, but they do not estimate revenue or observed compliance outcomes.
}\DIFaddend 

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{In our real time evaluation of the GRH inflow forecaststhere was one commercial forecast that stood out as having better performanceoverall. However, it was not always the best }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \subsection{\DIFadd{Comparison with retrospective forecast evaluation}}

\DIFadd{Hindcasts and real-time evaluations are complementary when they are designed as two parts of one comparison. A common hindcast can evaluate the same products across decades of hydrologic variability and, when coupled to a reservoir model, estimate effects on revenue, water supply, spill, and other outcomes. Separate vendor hindcasts are not necessarily comparable if they use different periods, model versions, meteorological inputs, infrastructure, or operating rules. A common experiment must hold these elements constant.
}

\DIFadd{The real-time trial supplies information that a hindcast usually omits. It tests products as they are delivered against the same current observations, and it records data interruptions, operator adjustments, current constraints, and the decisions that forecast errors affect. The limited period cannot establish long-term value or sample all hydrologic regimes. Its role is to determine which products are operationally viable and what a subsequent hindcast must represent. If comparable hindcasts already exist, the two evaluations can proceed in parallel. Otherwise, the real-time trial should precede the value study so that the hindcast uses current constraints and decision rules rather than an outdated system representation.
}


\subsection{\DIFadd{From forecast quality to forecast value}}

\DIFadd{The metrics presented here measure forecast quality rather than forecast value. Quality describes the relation between forecasts and observations. Value also depends on the decision, its consequences, and the decision maker's tolerance for risk. A more accurate forecast has no additional value if it does not change an action. Classical cost-loss analysis links forecast use to the utility of a decision \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{thompsonEconomicUtilityWeather1955}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. The relative utility value framework extends this approach to binary, multicategory, and continuous decisions and can represent risk aversion \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{laugesenFlexibleForecastValue2023}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citet{farkasEconomicValueWeather2025} }\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
review related methods.
}

\DIFadd{The tailored metrics are a step toward value because they express forecast error in units related to a decision. The forebay result does not demonstrate that compliance violations were avoided. Instead, it shows that a valuation cannot assume unrestricted use of the inflow forecasts: under the pass-inflow stress test, cumulative error frequently exhausts the operating band and requires operator intervention. A subsequent hindcast-based value study should therefore admit only schedules that satisfy the forebay constraint and should represent how operators respond as the band is approached. At GRH, the real-time trial can first screen products and define these constraints. The selected forecasts can then drive a common scheduling and dispatch model containing reservoir constraints, unit performance, day-ahead and real-time prices, and the current operating strategy. Benefits should be measured relative to the forecast and decisions now in use and tested under different operating strategies and attitudes toward risk.
}

In this study we did not address how much }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{one deterministic forecast for all conditions. Product selection should account for the decisions, seasons, and horizons that matter most to the operator. Probabilistic forecasts can describe uncertainty around these choices, although operators must still submit fixed market bids.
}

\DIFadd{The results also show why forecast performance should not be compared among locations using raw efficiency metrics alone. NSE and KGE depend on the distribution of observations, so a high score at one site does not necessarily imply smaller errors than a lower score at another site. RMSE and reference forecasts provide additional context. Comparisons among products should also use the same dates when their coverage differs. Here, restricting the analysis to the common period did not change the product rankings.
}

\DIFadd{We did not estimate how }\DIFaddend improvements in inflow forecasts \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{will translate into improvements in hydropower operations and increased revenue. While this seems a straight forward next steps, many hydropower scheduling models work with ranges of operations that match the utility's bidding strategy and local market opportunities. This complexity implies that evaluating the opportunities for harnessing higher forecast accuracy may also requires updating scheduling models and exploring more monetized operations}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{affect hydropower operations or revenue. A model configured to reproduce the current bidding strategy may understate the benefit of a more accurate forecast because that strategy was developed around the incumbent product. Estimating value may therefore require changes to the scheduling model and operating strategy as well as the forecast input}\DIFaddend .

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Real time forecast evaluations are inherently limited by the amount of time they are conducted. Ideally a forecast model's performance would be assessed over the full range of hydrologic conditionsspanning multiple decades. A hindcast evaluation may account for the full }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The length of the available record also limits the evaluation. The period included a regional flood and a rain-on-snow event (Section \ref{sec:period}), but it did not include a severe multi-year drought. We could not assess forecast performance under sustained water-limited conditions. A multi-decade hindcast would include a wider }\DIFaddend range of hydrologic \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{variability but this requires an operations model or considerable effort on the part }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{conditions and provide a more complete evaluation of forecast quality.
}

\DIFadd{The choice between real-time and retrospective evaluation depends on the needs }\DIFaddend of the system \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{operators to re-operate the system for every hindcast. The decision to pursue a real time or hindcast evaluation must be made by the system operators depending on their specific system needs and resource availability}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and the available resources. We recommend conducting both when feasible. For a system with strong seasonal variation, a real-time evaluation should span at least one year. A shorter period samples only part of the annual cycle, and its conclusions remain conditional on the seasons and events observed}\DIFaddend .

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{The iterative nature of this framework is perhaps its most valuable component. Having direct operator involvement and feedback helps to provide value to }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Two other limitations affect the interpretation of the results. }

The development of tailored metrics make the results relevant to a particular system and helps with operator buy-in. Furthermore, both generic and tailored metrics can ultimately be extended to assess the impacts of inflow forecasts on hydropower scheduling. Particularly under dynamic electricity market conditions, evaluations of inflow forecasts can serve as key indicators for understanding how forecasting errors propagate through the system, influencing compliance with operational requirements, variations in actual power generation, and potential revenue for system operators. The analysis of inflow forecast performance, conducted in coordination with system operators , represents an important foundational step toward achieving these broader applications. In presenting this framework we hope to provide a template for further applications and unbiased evaluation of the myriad of inflow forecasts available to hydropower systemoperators}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{framework allows the evaluation to change as operators identify which forecast errors affect their decisions.
